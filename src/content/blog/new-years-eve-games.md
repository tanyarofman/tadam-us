Not every New Year's Eve needs a ticket to a party. A night at home with the people you spent the year with can be the one you remember, as long as there's more to it than the TV and a countdown. Here are 15 New Year's Eve games, 20 questions about your year (not the year in the news), and a plan for the whole evening.

## When is New Year's Eve 2026?

New Year's Eve 2026 falls on Thursday, December 31, and 2027 begins at midnight. New Year's Day, Friday, January 1, is a federal holiday, so most guests can stay up late.

A Thursday night means many people have work on the morning of December 31, so plan for guests to arrive around 7 or 8 p.m. With kids at home, you can still hold an early countdown and keep the real one for the adults.

## What can you do on New Year's Eve at home?

Build the evening in three parts: food, a game or two about the year, and the countdown. The plan below works for family or friends, and you can copy it as it is.

| Time | What you do | How long |
|---|---|---|
| 8:00 p.m. | Dinner, everyone brings a dish | 1 hour |
| 9:00 p.m. | Two truths and a lie about the year | 15 min |
| 9:30 p.m. | Year-in-review quiz | 20 min |
| 10:30 p.m. | Awards of the year | 30 min |
| 11:30 p.m. | Resolutions and time capsule | 20 min |
| 11:55 p.m. | Countdown | 5 min |

With young kids, move everything three hours earlier and count down at 9. Pick games that fit your group: two or three are enough.

## What are fun New Year's Eve games for adults?

The best adult games use the year everyone just lived through: what happened, what made them laugh, and what they're planning. These six work with friends, family or a mix.

1. **Year-in-review quiz.** Everyone sends three facts about their year in advance, and you turn them into questions: "Which of us moved twice this year?"
2. **Two truths and a lie about the year.** Share three things that happened to you this year, one of them made up. Everyone guesses the lie.
3. **Photo of the year.** Everyone sends one photo that sums up their year. Show them without names, guess whose each one is, then hear the story.
4. **Awards of the year.** Vote on funny categories: longest voice memo, best excuse, dish of the year, most dramatic haircut.
5. **Year bingo.** Each square is something someone might have done this year: "started a class," "visited a new state," "changed jobs." Find who in the room did each one.
6. **Name that song of the year.** Play two seconds of songs that soundtracked your group's year, and guess.

## Which New Year's Eve games work for families with kids?

Kids need games with movement, short turns and a reason to stay awake. These five work from about age five, and the whole family can play together.

7. **Countdown balloons.** Before the party, put a small task on a slip of paper inside a balloon for every hour until the countdown. Pop one on the hour: a dance-off, a joke, a family photo.
8. **Early midnight.** Count down at 8 or 9 p.m. with noisemakers and confetti, then the kids go to bed happy.
9. **Kids' awards.** Kids choose the categories and hand out paper medals: best hugger, funniest sibling, best pancake flipper.
10. **New Year's charades.** Act out things from the year: "learning to ride a bike," "the first day of school," "the family road trip."
11. **The year in drawings.** Everyone draws their favorite moment of the year, and the others guess what it is.

## Which New Year's Eve games work for couples?

For two people, the best games are conversations with a twist: memories from the year and predictions for the next. These four work at home with a bottle of something good.

12. **Our year in 12 months.** Take turns naming one moment from each month of the year. If you're stuck, scroll your phone's photos.
13. **Predict my answer.** Write down how your partner will answer a question, then compare: "What was my best day this year?"
14. **Two-person awards.** Choose the meal, trip, show and argument of the year together.
15. **Sealed predictions.** Each of you writes three predictions for the other's year, seals them in an envelope, and opens it next New Year's Eve.

## What year-in-review questions should you ask?

Ask questions about everyone's own year, not the news. Everyone answers the same question, because comparing answers is where the fun is.

1. What was your best day this year?
2. What's something you tried for the first time?
3. What's the funniest thing that happened to you?
4. What's one thing you're proud of?
5. What was your most-played song?
6. What's the best thing you ate?
7. Where's the best place you went?
8. What's the best decision you made?
9. What's a skill you learned?
10. Who's someone new you met?
11. What surprised you most this year?
12. What did you stop doing, and don't miss?
13. Which photo on your phone sums up your year?
14. What's the best book, show or podcast you found?
15. What's a moment you'd like to relive?
16. What's one thing you'd do differently?
17. Who in this room made your year better, and how?
18. What's the most-used word in your texts this year?
19. What are you most looking forward to next year?
20. If your year were a movie, what would it be called?

For a group that doesn't know each other well, start with a few lighter [icebreaker questions](/blog/icebreaker-questions/) first.

## How do you turn New Year's resolutions into a game?

Have everyone write one resolution on a slip of paper without their name, then read them out and guess whose each one is. Seal them in an envelope and open it next New Year's Eve to see who kept theirs.

The wording matters more than you'd think. A [study of more than 1,000 people in PLOS ONE](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7725288/) found that people whose resolutions were about starting something ("start running") succeeded more often than those whose resolutions were about quitting something ("stop eating sweets"): 58.9% against 47.1% after a year. So add a house rule: every resolution has to start with "start" or "more."

> **Example scenario (not a real event):** Twelve friends in the living room at 10:30 p.m. The awards are tied for "excuse of the year" until someone reads out a resolution: "Start answering voice memos within a week." Everyone looks at the same person, and the tie is broken.
