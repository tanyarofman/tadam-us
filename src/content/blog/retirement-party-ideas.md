A retirement party marks the end of decades of work and the start of something new. The best ones feel less like a farewell and more like a celebration: stories from the people who worked alongside the retiree, a few laughs and a look at what comes next. Here are 25 ideas, games for coworkers and family, and what to say.

## What usually happens at a retirement party?

Most retirement parties have food and drinks, a few speeches from colleagues and the boss, a gift, and a short speech from the retiree. Many add a slideshow of their career or a game about their years at work.

A typical order for an office party:

1. **Arrivals and food.** Lunch, appetizers or cake and coffee.
2. **Speeches.** The boss, a longtime colleague and a friend, two or three minutes each.
3. **A game or a slideshow.** Ten to fifteen minutes about the retiree's career.
4. **The gift.** From the whole team.
5. **The retiree's speech.** Short thanks, often the most moving moment of the party.

People retire at different ages: Gallup found that [retirees report retiring at 61 on average, while workers expect to retire at 66](https://news.gallup.com/poll/394943/retiring-planning-retire-later.aspx). Either way, the party usually marks decades of work.

## Who usually throws a retirement party?

The employer or the team usually throws the office party, often organized by a manager or a close colleague. Family or friends often throw a second, more personal party at home or in a restaurant.

- **At work:** a manager, HR or the retiree's closest colleagues. The company often pays for food and a gift.
- **With family:** a spouse, children or close friends.
- **Both:** many retirees have an office lunch with coworkers and a bigger party with family on the weekend.

If you're organizing the office party, ask the retiree what they want. Some love a big send-off; others would rather have a quiet lunch with their team.

## What games can you play at a retirement party?

Play games about the retiree: trivia about their career, a guess-the-year round with old photos, two truths and a lie, or a bucket list game. They work with coworkers and family, and give guests from different parts of their life something to share.

**For coworkers:**

- **Career trivia (16).** Questions about the retiree's years at work: their first project, their famous email sign-off, the office legend only they know.
- **Guess the year.** Show old photos of the retiree at work, and guests guess the year.
- **Who said it.** Quotes from the retiree's emails and meetings over the years.
- **Retirement bingo.** Squares like "mentions golf", "says they'll still check email" and "someone cries".

**For family and friends:**

- **How well do you know the retiree.** Their first job, their dream job as a kid, the place they want to visit first.
- **Two truths and a lie.** About their career, told by the retiree.
- **Bucket list guessing.** Guests guess what's at the top of the retiree's list.

## How do you make a retirement party fun?

Keep the speeches short, add one game that gets everyone laughing and make the party about the retiree's stories, not about their age. A themed party around what comes next, like travel or golf, keeps the mood forward-looking.

- **Limit the speeches.** Three speakers, two or three minutes each.
- **Collect stories in advance.** Ask colleagues past and present for one story each.
- **Invite the old team.** Former colleagues who moved on often love being asked.
- **Look forward.** A bucket list board or a travel theme turns a goodbye into a launch party.
- **Play one game.** Ten to fifteen minutes, everyone in the room.

> **Example scenario (not a real event):** An office retirement party for 40 colleagues. The quiz asks what the retiring engineer wrote in his first email to the team in 1998. The younger colleagues guess "hello world". His longtime manager remembers the real answer: "Who took my stapler", and the room that has heard him ask it for 25 years laughs for a full minute.

## What should you say at a retirement party?

Tell one specific story about the retiree, say what they meant to the team, and raise a glass to what comes next. Keep it to two or three minutes, and leave the retiree the last word.

[Toastmasters suggests keeping a toast to two or three minutes](https://www.toastmasters.org/resources/public-speaking-tips/how-to-give-a-toast). A simple structure:

1. **One story.** A moment that shows who they are at work.
2. **What they gave the team.** Advice, patience, a sense of humor.
3. **What comes next.** The trip, the grandchildren, the garden.
4. **The toast.** "To [name], and to the next chapter."

If you're the retiree, thank the people who helped you, tell one story about your early days, and keep it short. Everyone remembers the speech that made them laugh, not the long one.

## How do you make the night about their career story?

Collect stories, photos and answers from colleagues past and present, and turn them into a game everyone plays at once. It gives the retiree a chance to hear what they meant to people, and gives the guests a reason to share.
